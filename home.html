About

I'm Joe Elias, a doctoral student in the Ecology and Evolutionary Biology Department at Binghamton University, where I work in the Grames Lab for Insect Ecology and Conservation. Ultimately I seek to improve and leverage non‑lethal tools to monitor environmental change. Currently my work focuses on using historical bioacoustics data to inform insect conservation and developing computational methods using GIS data for optimized site selection to build monitoring networks from fine to broad scales.

Background

I hold a M.S. in Biology from Commonwealth University of Pennsylvania (2023) and a B.S. in Wildlife and Environmental Biology from Keystone College (2021). As my research has shifted my skillset has shifted with it—typically rooted in conservation management. During my B.S. I worked as a trail manager in a sugar bush where I later took on the role as lead maple sugaring producer in addition to trail manager. As I was finishing my B.S. I took a seasonal technician role at the USDA Forest Service where I learned bird identification, banding, and surveying techniques. I also surveyed streams for Wood Turtles at this time. I carried these skills with me to my next role surveying bird community response to forest management techniques. Working in these positions—where I could explore my interests, move quickly on ideas, and engage in the publication process—solidified my decision to pursue graduate school.

My love of birds and interest in using them to indicate environmental change led me to the ecotoxicology field, where my M.S. advisor, Dr. Clay Corbin and I developed a novel way to measure acute microplastic ingestion in terrestrial habitats using bird banding methods, and used it to compare against necropsy methods, and measuring microplastic loading across different landscapes. My M.S. research introduced a lot of questions about how to improve and generalize monitoring iniatives across conservation issues, and I also wanted to branch downward trophically to start considering insect communities which experience many of the same challenges as birds, just with far less attention! During this time I was also researching Saw-whet Owls using passive acoustic monitoring (PAM) to track their breeding activity and dipped my toes into audio analysis, as a bioacoustics technician for the University of Pitssburgh. The culmination of these questions, considerations, and interests brought me to the Grames Lab, where Dr. Grames and I have been really excited about developing and pursuing some of these questions related to improving and adding to environmental monitoring initiatives, and using bioacoustics to improve our understanding of sound-producing insect communities.

I view myself as a general ecologist and am committed to building the skills necessary to address any conservation challenge or research question that emerges. I am pursuing a career in teaching and/or research, either at an academic or other institution. Once strictly a field biologist, I've become comfortable both in the field collectiong data and behind a computer doing analysis or modeling. All of my datatsets and code for published work are open access—I am a big supporter of open-science practices. I mostly work in R, although I am slowly learning Python and Jupyter Notebook as needed, and have been dabbling in html (i.e., this website). All of my publications, posters, and presentations are posted on my ResearchGate for open access viewing. If you made it this far, thanks for visiting my page! Please feel free to reach out if I can be of any help to you, or connect with me through any of the channels listed on this website.
