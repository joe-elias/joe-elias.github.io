About

I'm Joe Elias, a doctoral student in the Ecology and Evolutionary Biology Department at Binghamton University, where I work in the Grames Lab for Insect Ecology and Conservation. Ultimately I seek to improve and leverage non‑lethal tools to monitor environmental change. Currently my work focuses on using historical bioacoustics data to inform insect conservation and developing computational methods using GIS data for optimized site selection to build large-scale monitoring networks.

Background

I hold a M.S. in Biology from Commonwealth University of Pennsylvania (2023) and B.S. in Wildlife and Environmental Biology from Keystone College (2021). As my research has shifted my skillset has shifted with it—typically rooted in conservation management. During my B.S. I worked as a trail manager in a sugar bush where I later took on the role as lead maple sugaring producer 🍁
. As I was finishing my B.S. I took a seasonal technician role at the USDA Forest Service where I learned bird identification, banding, and surveying techniques-I also surveyed Wood Turtles and Saw-whet Owls at this time 🐦🐢. I carried these skills with me to my next role surveying bird community response to forest management and silviculture techniques. Assisting with ongoing research in these positions solidified my desire to continue onto grad school. My love of birds and interest in using them to indicate environmental change led me to the ecotoxicology field, where my M.S. advisor, Dr. Clay Corbin and I developed a novel way to measure acute microplastic exposure in terrestrial habitats using common bird banding methods, and used it to compare against necropsy methods, and measuring microplastic loading across different landscapes. My M.S. research brought up a lot of questions about how to improve and generalize monitoring iniatives across conservation issues, and I also wanted to branch downward trophically to start considering insect communities, which are often going through everything that birds are going through, but with way less attention! During this time I also got back into Saw-whet research, using passive acoustic monitoring (PAM) to track their breeding activity and dipped my toes into audio analysis, as a bioacoustics technician for the University of Pitssburgh. The culmination of these questions, considerations, and interests brought me to the Grames Lab, where Dr. Grames and I have been really excited about developing and pursuing some of these questions related to improving environmental monitoring and using bioacoustics to improve our understanding of sound-producing insect communities.
