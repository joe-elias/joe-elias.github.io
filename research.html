🦗 Restoring a Historical Multi‑decade Time Series of Nocturnal Passive Acoustic Monitoring

Bioacoustics—the study of sound production within ecosystems—enables researchers to efficiently track ecological change by measuring the natural communication signals produced by wildlife. Although widely used for studying birds, bioacoustics remains underutilized for insects.

Orthopteraplay essential roles in nutrient cycling, food‑web support, and bioindication. Declines at an estimated rate of 2.1–2.7% per year in Orthoptera abundance threaten these ecosystem services, yet they remain far less studied than most other insect groups. One major barrier to routine passive acoustic monitoring (PAM) of sound‑producing insects is the scarcity of recordings collected under natural field conditions which is crucial for rapid and standardized species identification. I am working to restore and analyze a historical PAM dataset totaling ~7,000 hours (25 years) of consistent soundscape recordings replicated across eight sites. This project involves digitizing and restoring nine years of analog cassette recordings, conducting audio analysis and annotation, and customizing a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) to automate species detection. The resulting dataset will be used to infer long-term trends in Orthoptera biodiversity and potential environmental drivers of change.

🗺️📍 An Algorithmic Approach for Optimal Site Selection to Help Design Ecological Monitoring Networks

Monitoring networks are vital for tracking change within and between ecosystems. However, many monitoring networks have bias introduced at the research design phase which can skew inference. I am developing an algorithmic approach to quantify and account for bias during site selection under common geographic and environmental constraints for representative ecological monitoring initiatives.

The method can be used to establish new site networks as well as update and quantify the optimization of existing networks to help better understand the resulting data. This method uses existing GIS data and GIS tools in R (mostly terra and sf) that are typically open access. To help others apply my the method, I have developed an R package-siteselectr- which can be downloaded via my GitHub, or using devtools::install_github(). Accompanying the R package is a GUI option for the general application of this method developed as a ShinyApp.

🐦Microplastic Loading in Terrestrial Birds from Non‑Lethal Sampling

The accumulation of microplastics is a global problem that is increasing for a widening list of taxonomic groups and trophic levels. There is little research on microplastic loading in terrestrial birds despite their usefulness as indicators to measure the ecological effects of pollution by virtue of their diverse behavior and morphology, as well as their relative ease to study. We tested whether MP were present in terrestrial birds ranging in size from 7-36 cm via two methods of non-lethal sample collection: (1) gastrointestinal tract dissections using already-deceased specimens and (2) non-invasive fecal sampling via bird banding. We found that bird banding with diet analysis was a comparable non‑lethal approach to achieve larger datasets for immediate micrplastic ingestion. The GI tract results suggest long‑term retention in these species. We found that MP loads are more influenced by fine-scale local variation on individuals rather than ecological characteristics like diet or foraging strategy, or morphological variation like body size. This research is currently in review—stay tuned! For more information on the influence and trajectories of microplastics in avian communities, please read more in Elias and Corbin (2025).

🐟Vertical Water Column Position Determines Density and Types of Microplastics in a Lentic System

In deep stratified lakes, knowing the mechanisms determining the vertical distribution of microplastics is necessary to understand their total influence on aquatic food webs. We collected microplastics at seven depths in the water column and bottom lake sediments. We found that the the type and number of microplastics varied by depth and type, likely due to temperature-associated density differences and other natural lake processes. These findings may be crucial for understanding and mediating microplastic–organism interactions across aquatic communities that undergo vertical dispersal in lentic systems (e.g., nektonic fish or daily vertical migration like zooplankton). For more, please read Elias and Rier (2023).

🦉Documenting Northern Saw-Whet Owls Breeding in Atypical Mixed-Deciduous Forest.

Aegolius acadicus (Northern Saw-whet Owl) is an ecologically important, nocturnal, cryptic species that utilizes forests in Pennsylvania for stopover habitat, wintering, and breeding. The extent to which this species nests in the mixed-deciduous forests of northeastern Pennsylvania is uncertain. We used passive acoustic monitoring (PAM) and audio playbacks throughout a reclaimed state park in northeastern PA to assess potential breeding chronology and habitat-use patterns. We found that Saw-whet Owls vocalized at 60% of passive acoustic monitoring stations during the early nesting period, and playback surveys produced an additional 2 acoustic detections. Incidental observations detected 2 fledglings in the post-nesting period of the breeding season. We found a positive association between vocalization rates and canopy cover. Our results suggest that Saw-whet Owls are nesting in Pennsylvnaia habitat that is considered atypical for this species and their range. This research adds to a growing body of research that Saw-whet Owls have more breeding plasticity than originally believed.

🦉Abundant Natural Cavities in a Deciduous Forest Leads to a Lack of Nest-Box Occupancy by Northern Saw-Whet Owls.

Nest boxes are commonly used for species that utilize tree cavities, including small owls. We checked boxes set out for Northern Saw-whet Owls after a hiatus of 8 years and found 3 rodent nests, but no evidence of owl usage. Playbacks revealed owls were present at 8 of 11 boxes checked. Cavity surveys yielded an average of 7.7 large cavities within 50 m of each box; extrapolation of cavity densities to a minimal territory size of 150 ha suggested that suitable cavities are an abundant, non-limiting resource here. Cavity abundance was likely a consequence of beech bark disease complex having recently top-killed most large American Beech trees locally, providing abundant resources for excavating woodpeckers.
