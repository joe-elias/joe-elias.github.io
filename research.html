Measuring long-term trends in Orthopteran communities using historical bioacoustics data

Orthoptera (grasshoppers, crickets, and katydids) play key roles in nutrient cycling, food‑web support, and bioindication. Declines at an estimated rate of 2.1–2.7% per year in Orthoptera abundance threaten these ecosystem services, yet they remain far less studied than most other insect groups (Welti et al. 2020). Long-term data that do exist often capture only a part of the Orthoptera community. Traditional methods (i.e., sweep-netting surveys) often miss burrow or arboreal dwelling species and require extensive sampling effort. Passive acoustic monitoring (PAM) reduces this taxonomic bias and is easily replicated (Kohlberg, Meyers, and Figueroa 2024), but long-running PAM initiatives (>10 years) for Orthoptera do not exist. The longest Orthoptera PAM initiative ran for 6 years (Jeliazkov et al. 2016), too short to credibly characterize temporal trends (Cusser et al. 2021). One of the main hurdles for routine PAM for sound-producing insects is the lack of recordings collected under natural field conditions, which are essential for rapid and standardized species identification (Riede 2018). I propose to restore and reanalyze a historical PAM dataset totaling approximately ~7,000 hours (25 years) of recordings of Orthoptera stridulations replicated across eight sites. This project will involve digitizing and restoring nine years of analog (cassette) recordings, annotating a training dataset, and customizing a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) to run automated species detection across the full 25 years of data. The resulting dataset can be used to infer long-term trends in Orthoptera biodiversity and potential environmental drivers of change.
